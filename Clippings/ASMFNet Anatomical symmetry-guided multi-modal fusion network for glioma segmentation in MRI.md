## 1\. Introduction

Gliomas are among the most aggressive primary tumors of the central nervous system (CNS). Epidemiological data indicate that 94,390 new cases of brain tumors were diagnosed in the United States alone in 2023. Currently, approximately one million Americans face potential health consequences from brain tumors. Between 2017 and 2021, malignant brain and other CNS tumors contributed substantially to cancer-related mortality, indicating their impact on public health (). Furthermore, the prognosis for glioma patients remains poor. For instance, glioblastoma, the most common and aggressive glioma subtype, has a five-year relative survival rate of approximately 6.9%, with a median survival time of about 8 months (). These statistics highlight the critical need for improved diagnostic and therapeutic strategies to treat gliomas effectively. Accurate delineation of glioma boundaries and sub-regions, including edema, enhancing tumor, and necrotic/non-enhancing tumor core (NCR/NET), is critical for surgical planning, prognostic evaluation, and personalized therapeutic decision-making (). Brain glioma segmentation typically leverages four complementary MRI modalities: FLAIR, T2, T1, and T1c. An illustrative example is shown in. FLAIR and T2 sequences are particularly sensitive to fluid content. T1 offers clear anatomical detail, and T1c highlights regions with abnormal blood-brain barrier permeability, such as actively proliferating tumor tissue (, ). Additionally, severe class imbalance exists among tumor sub-regions, as small but clinically significant regions like enhancing tumors are often sparsely represented. This imbalance can bias models toward larger regions, such as edema, reducing sensitivity for detecting critical tumor components and potentially compromising segmentation reliability in clinical practice. Moreover, it can be observed that the glioma disrupts the natural symmetry of the brain, leading to asymmetries in structural pattern. These asymmetries can serve as strong cues for identifying tumor boundaries and sub-regions, especially when integrated with multi-modal imaging.

Glioma segmentation faces several challenges, including spatial heterogeneity in tumor morphology, labor-intensive process for manual annotation, and inter-observer variability (, ). These factors collectively lead to inconsistencies in region delineation and hinder the reliability of diagnostic outcomes. Previous glioma segmentation approaches include traditional image processing-based methods such as thresholding (), region growing (), level set methods (), and machine learning-based techniques such as support vector machines (SVM) () and particle swarm optimization (). However, these methods often struggle to adapt to the complexity and variability of real-world cases.

In contrast, deep learning-based methods have emerged as a promising paradigm for addressing these challenges. Various convolutional neural network (CNN) methods have been proposed for glioma segmentation, including U-Net and its variants (). To further improve segmentation performance, attention mechanism-based methods such as AttU-Net (), SF-Net (), ACMINet (), and TDPC-Net () have been developed. Subsequently, Transformer-based methods such as EoFormer (), NestedFormer (), SegFormer3D (), and Slim UNETR () have been proposed to better capture long-range dependencies in the data. These methods have guided networks to focus more on potentially ambiguous boundary locations, thereby improving segmentation accuracy in challenging regions.

Although current studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of multi-modal fusion, several critical challenges remain for clinical application. First, the ability to model disruptions in the symmetrical structure of healthy brain regions is limited. Second, during intra-modality feature extraction, the recognition of key anatomical features remains inefficient. Third, cross-modal feature fusion mechanisms lack effective attention guidance. Finally, class imbalance limits the detection of critical tumor components and weakens segmentation reliability in clinical practice. These limitations significantly constrain the generalization ability of existing methods in complex clinical scenarios. To address these challenges, we propose an Anatomical Symmetry-guided Multi-modal Fusion Network (ASMFNet) for precise glioma segmentation, which leverages anatomical symmetry priors to guide the network’s focus toward glioma regions. The proposed network enhances glioma-related features from each modality and optimizes feature fusion through cross-modal correlation analysis. The main contributions of this work include:
- 1.
	To address the disruption of healthy brain anatomical symmetry caused by gliomas, we introduce an anatomical symmetry guidance (ASG) module, which analyzes the asymmetry resulting from glioma presence and leverages this information to improve segmentation accuracy.
- 2.
	To direct the encoder toward the correct tumor regions during feature extraction, we propose an intra-modality purification (IMP) module, which enhances glioma-related features while suppressing irrelevant information.
- 3.
	To better exploit the complementary information across different modalities, we incorporate a cross-modal fusion (CMF) module in the decoder, which adaptively fuses features from multiple modalities to achieve more comprehensive and accurate segmentation.
- 4.
	To address both class imbalance and the clinical relevance of different tumor sub-regions, we propose an anatomically-informed segmentation loss function that assigns tailored weights based on the anatomical importance of each region, thereby improving training stability and segmentation accuracy.

This paper is organized as follows: reviews the related work. presents the proposed network architecture. describes the experimental setup and parameter settings. provides a detailed analysis of the experimental results. discusses and compares the alternative methods explored in this work and offers insights into potential future research directions. summarizes the work.

## 2\. Related work

### 2.1. Glioma segmentation

In recent years, glioma segmentation methods have advanced rapidly and can be broadly categorized into three types based on their underlying technology. The first category primarily relies on traditional image processing techniques. For example, proposed a fuzzy kernel seed selection based graph cut method. It adopts fuzzy clustering to automatically detect glioma-matched seeds and enhance boundary segmentation accuracy. proposed a morphology-region growing method, which uses seed selection, dilation and erosion for lightweight glioma segmentation. Although these methods have achieved some success, they have inherent limitations, such as being applicable only to specific tasks. Moreover, they struggle to capture the complex 3D topology of gliomas and exhibit poor robustness to noise. The second category is based on machine learning. For example, first introduced the Gray Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) into glioma segmentation, improving Haralick texture feature engineering. used a random forest to extract first-order, higher-order, and texture features, focusing on tumor regions to reduce computation and improve segmentation accuracy. However, these methods encounter representation bottlenecks in manual feature design, limiting their ability to capture the complex characteristics of gliomas. The third category is based on deep learning. For example, proposed Diff-UNet, which integrates a denoising diffusion model with a U-shaped architecture and a step-uncertainty fusion module, achieving superior glioma segmentation. introduced a multi-modal glioma segmentation network with a novel tri-attention fusion mechanism, consisting of a dual-attention fusion module and a correlation attention module. proposed a Swin Transformer-based semantic segmentation module and an CNN-based edge detection module for glioma segmentation. proposed to integrate Transformer architecture into a diffusion model for glioma segmentation, effectively mitigating diffusion variance and enhancing segmentation stability.

### 2.2. Anatomical symmetry

Brain tissue exhibits approximate symmetry at the macroscopic level, and lesions such as gliomas often disrupt this symmetry. Therefore, incorporating anatomical symmetry as a prior into models has become an important direction in brain glioma segmentation. For instance, proposed explicitly encoding symmetry into existing segmentation networks, encouraging the model to focus more on regions that are inconsistent after mirroring. This approach demonstrated higher data utilization efficiency and faster convergence compared to the original model on the BraTS dataset. Similarly, introduced a symmetry-difference map as an additional input to guide the network toward asymmetric regions and improve segmentation performance. developed a Deep Convolutional Symmetric Neural Network (DCSNN) that incorporates left-right similarity masks into DCNN layers, enabling localization of asymmetric brain regions and achieving competitive results on the BRATS dataset. In the unsupervised paradigm, proposed to learn nonlinear mappings between the left and right hemispheres. They used symmetric reconstruction to generate a normal brain and comparing the error with the original image to indicate lesions. More recently, proposed the Attentive Symmetric Autoencoder (ASA), a Vision Transformer-based framework that applies gradient-based attention to informative patches and symmetric position encoding to capture long-range correlations. proposed a hybrid CNN for glioma segmentation, which incorporates a symmetric encoder path to capture asymmetric features and emphasize tumor locations. These approaches collectively highlight the value of symmetry priors in glioma segmentation, though challenges remain in handling anatomical variations and computational efficiency for real-time clinical applications.

### 2.3. Attention mechanism

Recent advancements in attention mechanisms have offered valuable insights for medical image analysis. For example, introduced the Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) network, which pioneered channel attention by generating channel weights through global average pooling, significantly enhancing ImageNet classification performance. further enhanced the SE network by introducing the Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM), which incorporates a spatial attention module to form a unified channel-spatial attention mechanism. proposed the Efficient Pyramid Squeeze Attention (EPSA) block and its backbone architecture EPSANet, which enhance multi-scale feature representation and channel dependencies via adaptive attention, allowing flexible integration into different networks. proposed a multi-axis attention mechanism that achieves linear complexity by decomposing spatial dimension computations. proposed a multi-modal glioma segmentation framework that leverages cross-modality self-attention, multi-scale feature fusion, and multi-task learning to exploit inter-modality correlations and recover missing data, enabling efficient glioma segmentation. proposed CIHM, a hybrid Mamba-CNN architecture with dual-branch feature fusion to balance local detail extraction and long-range context modeling. However, existing attention mechanisms in medical imaging applications face several limitations. They often fail to integrate anatomical prior knowledge, limiting their ability to leverage inherent structural information. Additionally, cross-modal attention fusion tend to be inefficient, restricting the model’s capacity to effectively fuse complementary information from different modalities. Furthermore, these mechanisms struggle to capture long-range dependencies in 3D volumetric data, which is crucial for comprehensive spatial understanding. To overcome these challenges, this paper proposes three novel modules to leverage anatomical symmetry information to achieve efficient cross-modal fusion. These modules effectively address the limitations of existing attention mechanisms, thereby enhancing both performance and robustness in glioma segmentation.

## 3\. Methodology

### 3.1. Motivation

Previous glioma segmentation methods often neglect the prior knowledge of anatomical symmetry, reducing the effectiveness of attention mechanisms in integrating multi-modal data. To address these challenges, this work proposes a novel Anatomical Symmetry-guided Multi-modal Fusion Network (ASMFNet). First, considering the anatomical symmetry disruption caused by glioma, we propose an Anatomical Symmetry Guidance (ASG) module to leverage this information to guide glioma segmentation. Second, since attention within modalities varies across channels, we design an Intra-Modality Purification (IMP) module to exploit channel correlations within each modality. Third, recognizing that each MRI modality has distinct characteristics, and provides complementary information about glioma location, we propose a Cross-Modal Fusion (CMF) module. This module groups the four modalities into teacher-student pairs and employs a novel fusion method to effectively integrate and leverage information across modalities. Recognizing the challenge of class imbalance and the varying clinical importance of tumor sub-regions, we finally design an anatomically informed loss function that applies tailored weights to each region, leading to more stable training and improved segmentation results.

### 3.2. The architecture of the proposed ASMFNet

The proposed ASMFNet adopts a four-stage encoder-decoder architecture, which is presented in. It includes three novel modules: an anatomical symmetry guidance (ASG) module to detect anatomical symmetry disruption, an intra-modality purification (IMP) module to achieve important feature extraction, and a cross-modal fusion (CMF) module to enable cross-modal feature fusion. First of all, four independent encoders are designed to process FLAIR, T2, T1 and T1c modalities, respectively. Each modality-specific encoder employs a Max-pooling and an ASG module in each stage. The ASG module enables the analysis of structural differences between the left and right brain regions during the encoding process, allowing the network to focus on anatomical abnormalities caused by tumors. Furthermore, an IMP module is integrated to the skip connections to further enhance the feature representation. The output of the IMP module is concatenated with the upsampled features followed by double 3 × 3 × 3 convolutions and fed into the next stage. The unified decoder consists of a CMF module followed by an upsampling at each stage. Specifically, the CMF module can dynamically compute cross-modal feature correlations and achieve adaptive feature fusion. Finally, segmentation probability maps are generated through double 3 × 3 × 3 convolutions and a Sigmoid activation function. The complete method of ASMFNet is presented in.

### 3.3. Anatomical symmetry guidance module (ASG)

To leverage the inherent symmetry of brain structures, we design an anatomical symmetry guidance (ASG) module to analyze the patterns of anatomical symmetry disruption in the brain. The architecture of the proposed ASG module is shown in. The ASG module consists of two parallel paths, one for maintaining the original feature information and the other for obtaining the symmetric feature information. In the first path, the input feature $f_{i n} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times D \times H \times W}$ is passed through Max-pooling to reduce the feature size, resulting in $fin′∈RC×12D×12H×12W$. Then, double 3 × 3 × 3 convolutions are employed to extract features leading to a new feature $fin″∈R2C×12D×12H×12W$. In the second path, four dilated convolutions with different dilation rates are first employed to capture multi-scale feature information. The outputs are then concatenated and passed through a 1 × 1 × 1 convolution to reduce the number of channels. The result of this convolution is denoted as $f_{a} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times D \times H \times W}$. Following this, sagittal mirror generation is performed on *f <sub>a</sub>* to obtain a symmetric representation, denoted as $fa′∈RC×D×H×W$. It is noted that the sagittal plane provides the most consistent and biologically meaningful symmetry, as it reflects the natural left-right hemispheric correspondence. Therefore, sagittal mirror generation is adopted to ensure reliable and robust symmetry representation. Next, both *f <sub>a</sub>* and $fa′$ are passed through the intra-modality purification module (IMP) (details provided in ) to obtain enhanced features, denoted as $f_{b} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times D \times H \times W}$ and $fb′∈RC×D×H×W$, respectively. Finally, the output $f_{c} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times \frac{1}{2} D \times \frac{1}{2} H \times \frac{1}{2} W}$ can be obtained by Max-pooling the result of the subtraction between *f <sub>b</sub>* and $fb′$. The output *f <sub>c</sub>* and the result from the first path are concatenated and passed through a 1 × 1 × 1 convolution to produce the final output $f_{o u t} \in \mathbb{R}^{2 C \times \frac{1}{2} D \times \frac{1}{2} H \times \frac{1}{2} W}$.

The main advantage of the proposed ASG module lies in embedding prior anatomical knowledge of brain symmetry into the network, enabling more effective identification of tumor-related abnormalities and improving segmentation performance. In addition, the ASG module employs multi-scale dilated convolutions to capture contextual information at different receptive fields, thereby enhancing long-range dependency modeling and feature representation. One potential concern is that the ASG module compares symmetry between the left and right hemispheres, which implicitly assumes that the input MRI is well preprocessed and the mid-sagittal plane is strictly centered. In real clinical scenarios, patient head posture may introduce slight tilting or rotation. To mitigate this, standard preprocessing pipelines, such as affine registration to a common template, are applied to ensure consistent alignment. Moreover, the deep network itself exhibits a certain degree of tolerance to small misalignments, making the symmetry analysis sufficiently robust for practical applications.

### 3.4. Intra-modality purification module (IMP)

To integrate feature information across channels, we propose an intra-modality purification (IMP) module. The architecture of the proposed IMP module is illustrated in. First, the input feature is processed to obtain feature $f_{a} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times 48 \times 48 \times 48}$ by applying max-pooling only if the input feature size exceeds 48 × 48 × 48; if the size is exactly 48 × 48 × 48 or 24 × 24 × 24, no operation is performed and the feature size is kept unchanged at either $\mathbb{R}^{C \times 48 \times 48 \times 48}$ or $\mathbb{R}^{C \times 24 \times 24 \times 24}$. Then, the feature *f <sub>a</sub>* passed to two parallel FC layers which are combined via matrix multiplication to achieve the initial channel correlation weight $W_{1} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times C}$, where each element $W_{1_{i j}}$ quantifies the pairwise dependency between channels *i*  and  *j* in the feature map. To mitigate the inaccuracy caused by channel self-correlations, we derive the final channel correlation weights $W_{2} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times 1}$ through a three-step refinement of the initial correlation matrix $W_{1} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times C}$: First, we eliminate self-correlations by applying diagonal masking ($W1′=W1⊙(1−I)$), where I is an identity matrix; then aggregate cross-channel dependencies via row-wise summation ($W1″=∑jW1ij′$); and finally apply Softmax normalization to obtain the attention weights ($W2=Softmax(W1″)$). This approach ensures the resulting weights focus on meaningful inter-channel relationships while suppressing self-referential biases, leading to more robust feature representations. Next, we multiply *f <sub>a</sub>* by the weight *W* <sub>2</sub> to obtain the refined feature map $fa′∈RC×48×48×48$ (or $fa′∈RC×24×24×24$). Finally, the feature map is restored to its original size $fin′∈RC×D×H×W$ through interpolation and added with the original input feature *f <sub>in</sub>* to obtain the final refined feature *f <sub>out</sub>*.

Additionally, the use of diagonal zeroing helps mitigate inaccuracy caused by channel self-correlation. Furthermore, the lightweight architecture ensures a reduction in computational complexity.

### 3.5. Cross-modal fusion module (CMF)

In order to effectively integrating modality-specific information, we propose a cross-modal fusion (CMF) module. The proposed network architecture is presented in. Clinical diagnostic experience indicates that FLAIR and T1c modalities are typically regarded as the primary diagnostic references because of their higher pathological specificity in highlighting tumor-related abnormalities. In contrast, T1 and T2 modalities, while carrying less pathological detail, provide crucial anatomical context that complements lesion characterization. Motivated by this clinical insight, we design a teacher-student learning paradigm, in which FLAIR and T1c serve as teacher modalities to distill discriminative pathological features, while T1 and T2 act as student modalities, receiving guided knowledge to strengthen their anatomical feature representations. The proposed CMF module consists of four stages. The first stage is designed to enhance the teacher modalities, where FLAIR and T1c undergo a 3 × 3 × 3 convolution, respectively, doubling their channel dimensions to obtain $f_{F} , f_{T 1 c} \in \mathbb{R}^{\frac{2}{4} C \times D \times H \times W}$. The second stage is designed to facilitate the teacher-student fusion. FLAIR is concatenated with T2, followed by Max-pooling and a 1 × 1 × 1 convolution to compress the feature map to $\frac{C}{8}$ channels, resulting in $f_{F T 2} \in \mathbb{R}^{\frac{1}{8} C \times D \times H \times W}$. Similarly, T1c is combined with T1 to generate $f_{T 1 c T 1} \in \mathbb{R}^{\frac{1}{8} C \times D \times H \times W}$ following the same process. The third stage focuses on the cross-modal attention generation. First, *f* <sub><em>FT</em> 2</sub> and *f* <sub><em>T</em> 1 <em>cT</em> 1</sub> are concatenated and then processed with a 3 × 3 × 3 convolution followed by Sigmoid activation function. Finally, the attention weight $f_{A t t} \in \mathbb{R}^{\frac{1}{2} C \times D \times H \times W}$ are generated. The fourth stage adopts the feature reconstruction mechanism, designed to mitigate information loss through a two-path strategy. In the first path, the input features $f_{i n} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times D \times H \times W}$ undergo channel-wise Max-pooling and the size is reduce to $fin′∈R12C×D×H×W$. Simultaneously, in the second path, *f <sub>in</sub>* is processed via a 1 × 1 × 1 convolution, reducing it to $fin″∈R12C×D×H×W$. The final output is obtained through a residual connection, formulated as:$fout=(fAtt⊗fin′)⊕fin″,$where ⊗ denotes element-wise multiplication, and ⊕ denotes element-wise addition.

The proposed CMF offers two key advantages. First, it emphasizes dominant feature extraction from high-information (teacher) modalities, ensuring their fully contribution to the segmentation task. Second, it introduces a phased compression-expansion mechanism for channel dimensions, effectively utilizing low-information (student) modalities. In addition, the module employs a multi-stage progressive fusion architecture with a 4-2-1-1 channel compression-expansion strategy, ensuring effective fusion between teacher and student modalities.

### 3.6. Loss function

The mean Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) is a primary performance metric for glioma segmentation, which is typically computed as the average scores across whole tumor (WT), tumor core (TC), and enhancing tumor (ET) regions. However, these classes exhibit significant anatomical differences in the BraTS dataset: WT includes ET, NCR/NET, and edema; TC comprises ET and NCR/NET; whereas ET is evaluated independently. Considering their anatomical characteristics and varying segmentation difficulty, we propose an anatomically-informed segmentation loss function, defined in. ## 4\. Experimental setup

### 4.1. Dataset and implementation details

The proposed method is evaluated on the Brain Tumor Segmentation Challenge 2018, 2019, and 2021 datasets (, ), containing multi-modal MRI scans from 285, 335, and 1251 glioma patients, respectively. Each case includes four MRI modalities (FLAIR, T1, T1c, and T2) with pixel-level annotations. Following the BraTS protocol, segmentation is performed on three tumor regions: Enhancing Tumor (ET), Tumor Core (TC), and Whole Tumor (WT). All images are center-cropped to 192  ×  192 × 192, normalized using Z-score normalization, and augmented with Gaussian noise during training. The proposed network model is implemented using the PyTorch framework and trained on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 (24GB) GPU. The AdamW optimizer is employed with an initial learning rate of 1e-4. To regulate the learning rate, a cosine annealing strategy is adopted, with each cosine cycle consisting of 50 epochs. Additionally, an early stopping mechanism is incorporated, terminating training if no new minimum validation loss is observed over 50 consecutive epochs. Furthermore, the batch size is set to 1, and the dataset is randomly partitioned into training and test sets in an 8:2 ratio.

### 4.2. Evaluation metrics

To comprehensively evaluate the performance of the proposed method, we employ two evaluation metrics for quantitative analysis: the Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) and the 95% Hausdorff Distance (HD95). The DSC quantifies the overlap between the predicted segmentation and the ground truth annotation, as shown in. The HD measures the maximum spatial discrepancy between the predicted tumor boundary and the ground truth boundary, formulated as:(3) $HD=max{maxx∈Xminy∈Yd(x,y),maxy∈Yminx∈Xd(x,y)},$where *X* and *Y* denote the predicted tumor boundary and the true tumor boundary, respectively, and *d* (*x, y*) represents the Euclidean distance.

## 5\. Experimental results and analysis

### 5.1. Ablation study

To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed module, the ablation study is conducted on the BraTS 2018, BraTS 2019 and BraTS 2021 datasets, respectively. We define the proposed method without any additional modules as the baseline method. Subsequently, each module is individually added to the baseline, and the corresponding results are presented in,,. From, it can be observed that the baseline method achieves 82.5% in terms of average DSC and 5.64 mm in terms of average HD95. Compared to the baseline method, the model incorporating the ASG module in the encoder achieves an increase of 0.97% in terms of average DSC and 8.87% in terms of average HD95. This improvement indicates that the proposed ASG module efficiently guides the network to focus on the asymmetric regions within the image, thereby enhancing segmentation performance. When the IMP module is integrated into the skip connections, the average DSC and HD95 are further improved by 0.85% and 6.91%, respectively, compared to the baseline. This enhancement is attributed to the IMP module’s ability to refine the feature maps within each modality. Finally, integrating the CMF module into the decoder leads to an additional 0.85% increase in terms of average DSC and 1.95% in terms of average HD95 relative relative to the baseline. This optimization is driven by the modal fusion mechanism in the decoder, which enhances the importance of the teacher modality, while the compression-expansion design in the CMF module allows crucial information to be more effectively expressed. These results demonstrate that adding any of the proposed modules to the baseline substantially improves the segmentation performance. Moreover, integrating any two modules can also yield consistent performance gains, confirming their strong complementarity and mutual reinforcement. In the final evaluation, the model incorporating all three modules achieves the most significant improvement over the baseline, with the average DSC increases by 2.30% and the average HD95 improving by 24.29%. Similar conclusions can be drawn on the BraTS 2019 and 2021 datasets in and. Moreover, due to the increased size of the dataset, the overall segmentation performance is significantly improved.

## 6\. Discussion

### 6.1. Analysis on the module architecture in ASG

We perform a comprehensive comparative analysis of two ASG architectural variants to determine the optimal configuration: a standard convolution-based design and an enhanced version with dilated convolution groups. In addition, we further investigate three attention mechanisms for feature refinement: CBAM (), PSA (), and IMP (Ours). The results in and show that the dilated convolution architecture achieves notably better segmentation performance, although CBAM slightly reduces DSC and PSA causes a minor increase in HD95. With dilated convolution, PSA exhibits the weakest performance, while both CBAM and IMP improve segmentation accuracy. However, CBAM requires substantially higher computational cost, running 3–5 times slower per epoch than our method. Thus, the final ASG design integrates dilated convolutions with the IMP mechanism, achieving a balance between segmentation accuracy and efficiency.

### 6.4. Limitations and future work

Although our method advances state-of-the-art glioma segmentation, three key limitations remain and guide future work. First, current approaches, including ours, tend to generate overly smooth tumor boundaries compared to ground truth annotations, particularly in small or diffuse regions (e.g., NCR/NET). Future work could integrate boundary-aware losses with hierarchical context aggregation to better capture fine anatomical details. Second, although we employ four modality-specific encoders, our fusion strategy does not fully leverage clinical priors. Incorporating an attention-based weighting mechanism guided by tumor characteristics could dynamically prioritize modalities (e.g., T1c for ET, FLAIR for WT), enhancing subtype-specific segmentation. Third, our current framework provides limited insight into model uncertainty, which is critical for clinical decision-making. Future work could integrate uncertainty estimation techniques, such as Monte Carlo dropout to quantify segmentation confidence and improve reliability, particularly in ambiguous or low-quality imaging regions. These improvements could bridge the gap between network performance and clinical applicability, particularly in low-resource settings.

## 7\. Conclusion

This paper proposes a novel glioma segmentation approach, termed the Anatomical Symmetry-guided Multi-modal Fusion Network (ASMFNet). By embedding an Anatomical Symmetry Guidance (ASG) module in the encoder, this approach incorporates prior knowledge of brain anatomical symmetry into the network architecture. Additionally, the proposed Intra-modality Purification (IMP) module, integrated into the skip connections, enables the encoder to learn useful feature information while suppressing irrelevant information. Finally, the Cross-modal Fusion (CMF) module integrates modality-specific features from each encoder, effectively preserving critical information and substantially improving segmentation accuracy. Extensive ablation and comparative experiments on the BraTS 2018, 2019, and 2021 datasets demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method. Moreover, the proposed method are not limited to glioma segmentation and can be generalized to multi-modal segmentation tasks for other applications.
